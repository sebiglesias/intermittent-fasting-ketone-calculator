⚠️ Clinical Safety & Absolute Contraindications

Absolute Medical Contraindications: Intermittent and prolonged fasting are strictly contraindicated for individuals with Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (risk of life-threatening diabetic ketoacidosis / DKA), history of clinical eating disorders (anorexia nervosa, bulimia), pregnancy, lactation, and growing adolescents.

Ketoacidosis vs. Nutritional Ketosis: Nutritional ketosis achieved through fasting typically produces serum beta-hydroxybutyrate levels between 0.5 and 3.5 mmol/L with normal blood glucose. Diabetic ketoacidosis involves dangerous unregulated ketone spikes (> 10.0 mmol/L) accompanied by severe hyperglycemia and metabolic acidosis.

Electrolyte Depletion: Prolonged fasting lowers circulating insulin, leading to renal natriuresis (accelerated excretion of sodium and water). Supplementing elemental sodium, potassium, and magnesium prevents the "keto flu" and orthostatic hypotension.

Frequently Asked Questions

When does cellular autophagy begin during a fast?
In humans, cellular autophagy (the lysosomal degradation of damaged proteins and organelles) begins up-regulating as hepatic glycogen empties, typically around 16 to 24 hours of complete fasting, with peak expression observed between 36 and 48 hours.
What breaks a fast?
Any substantial caloric intake (> 30–50 calories), especially rapidly absorbed carbohydrates or proteins containing leucine, stimulates insulin and activates the mTOR pathway, temporarily attenuating autophagy and hepatic ketogenesis.
How can I tell if I am in nutritional ketosis?
A calibrated blood ketone meter testing capillary blood is the gold standard. A reading of 0.5 to 3.0 mmol/L beta-hydroxybutyrate confirms nutritional ketosis.
